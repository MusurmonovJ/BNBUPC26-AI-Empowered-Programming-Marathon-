# Lethe six minute interactive speech

Use the full twelve-slide showcase. Invite the audience to imagine a private memory and choose where the last light goes. Allow 90 seconds for the actual garden or the recorded fallback. Keep the final vote invitation brief. These are rehearsal targets; the deck never advances automatically.

The example life is fictional. Describe the outcome actually shown in the game. The slide interactions record a kind and a room choice only in memory; they do not run the garden simulation or tally audience votes.

## 0:00–0:15 A life becomes a garden

> Before I show you the garden, I want to give you a place in it. Lethe is my response to “Your Life as a World.” It turns the fragments of a life into flowers and relationships.

Stand still for the opening. Look at the audience. Let the picture settle before speaking.

## 0:15–0:40 A memory of your own

> Take a moment to think of a memory. You can keep it private. Perhaps it is something someone said, or a place you can still walk through in your mind. What kind of fragment is it? Keep that moment with you while we explore.

Leave five seconds for thought. Choose a kind if someone volunteers one. Never ask for a personal story.

## 0:40–1:05 A fragment takes root

> A memory begins as a seed. Its words become a flower, with a shape that belongs to that memory. When it opens, it can find other moments that share a person or a place. Growing the world begins with one fragment.

Click “show the planting” twice, leaving time to see each real capture. This is a visual preview. Show the actual bloom in the live garden or recording.

## 1:05–1:40 Grandma Mei’s words

> Here is one fragment from the fictional example life. Grandma Mei said, “You don’t have to be brave all the time.” A kitchen, a song, and these words might live in separate places on a phone. In this garden, you can see what they share. You can see Grandma Mei across them.

Read the quote warmly. Pause. Move your gaze from the flower to the audience rather than reading every label.

## 1:40–2:05 What the fragments share

> These threads make relationships visible. Shared people and places help memories hold each other up. Tending one sends some of that care to its neighbors. A new memory can strengthen a connection that was already part of the life.

Click “show the connection.” Point to the real thread. Speak about shared names, not physical closeness.

## 2:05–2:35 One light and your choice

> There is one light left in this episode. Would you tend Grandma Mei’s words, letting care reach her connected memories? Or would you tend the library rooftop, a place that matters on its own? Raise a hand for the one you would choose. I will carry your choice into the garden.

Ask once, then stop talking. Select the room’s choice. Accept either answer without praising one as correct. No tally or personal data is recorded.

## 2:35–4:05 The living garden

> Let’s see what that choice does. I will tend the memory you chose, plant a related fragment, and let a season pass. Watch the light reach its neighbors. Then watch the garden change.

Open the verified game. In the short route allow 60 seconds for real actions and silence. In the showcase allow 90 seconds. If using the rehearsed film, explain that it follows its own fixed choice. Never pretend the film responds to the room.

## 4:05–4:30 A season passes

> The garden changes. Some flowers keep their color. Some become outlines. Care and connection affect what remains, and the spaces that grow quiet become part of the world too.

Use the real outcome you just saw. The dusk button compares reference captures; it does not simulate the audience’s decision.

## 4:30–4:45 The quiet space

> A lost flower leaves a seed. Its original words remain, and we can return to it. For now, I leave the space quiet.

Hold four seconds of silence. If the room kept the rooftop alive, discuss another actual lost seed or loss in general. Do not claim their rooftop disappeared.

## 4:45–5:10 What holds this life

> When the camera rises, the world becomes a portrait. You can see the people and places that hold the memories together. Another person might care for different moments. Their attention would give this same life another shape.

Reveal the names only after the picture settles. Keep your voice quieter than at the opening.

## 5:10–5:35 A world you can make your own

> The example life is fictional. You can plant your own memories too. Flowers keep a stable identity, and shared names form relationships. Your personal garden lives in this browser. Planting works with local tagging, with an optional provider for better suggestions.

Keep this brief. Answer implementation questions afterward. Do not claim provider-enabled tagging keeps every word on the device.

## 5:35–6:00 What will you carry

> Return to the memory you imagined at the beginning. What would you grow around it? You cannot keep every moment in bloom. What you tend gives the garden its shape. Try Lethe. If it stays with you, I would appreciate your vote.

End on the audience’s own imagined memory. Say the voting invitation once. Hold the final view.

## Delivery and audience engagement

- Begin with the room, then the project. Give people time to think before asking for a response.
- Ask for raised hands once. Accept either memory without judging it. If the room stays quiet, say “I will choose Grandma Mei for this first visit,” then proceed.
- Move deliberately. Put the cursor down while the flower grows or fades. The scene needs time to be seen.
- Speak plainly during the rules, warmly during the quotation, and more quietly during the ending. Let silence carry the loss scene.
- When using the recording, say “This recorded visit follows my choice of Grandma Mei.” Do not imply it responds to the room.
- Rehearse with a timer. Shorten explanations if you overrun; preserve the bloom and four-second quiet hold.
- Put the verified live URL and real voting destination into the links dialog before the audience arrives. Open speaker view on your private display, then fullscreen the deck on the projected display.
- End with an invitation once. Give the audience a clear route to trying and voting, without promising contest results.
